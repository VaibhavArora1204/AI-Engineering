## 1. Correctness Collapse and CockpitHAT

**Source:** ByteDance — CockpitHAT paper (arxiv 2608.01805)
**Core problem:** High task-level accuracy hides catastrophic process-level failures. A system that's 96% "correct" on the surface can be 56% correct when you actually trace the execution path.

### What Correctness Collapse Actually Is

Here's the scenario that should terrify you if you're building multi-agent systems. Your agent pipeline reports near-perfect accuracy on your eval suite. Everything looks green. Then somebody traces the actual execution path and discovers that sub-agents are passing unverified outputs down the graph, errors are cascading silently, and the final answer happens to be right despite the entire middle being broken.

This isn't hypothetical. ByteDance documents a real incident: a Lynk&Co in-vehicle AI copilot achieved 99% intent accuracy on a "lights off" command — while creating a critical visibility hazard. The task metric said success. The physical world said failure.

The term for this is **Correctness Collapse**: state-of-the-art models degrade from 96% to 56% accuracy when you switch from outcome evaluation to dialectical (process-level) evaluation. The gap between "did the final answer match" and "was the reasoning path sound" is enormous.

### Why Text-Only Attribution Fails

Existing error attribution methods (Who&When, ECHO, AgentRx) operate exclusively on text traces. They use positional windows — "look at the 5 steps before the failure" — which conflates positional adjacency with causal dependency. Two steps next to each other in time might have nothing to do with each other, while the actual cause might be 20 steps back in a completely different sub-thread.

In embodied settings (cars, robots, physical systems), this gets worse. A lexically correct utterance can trigger a hazardous physical action that's only visible through vehicle-state channels, environmental context, or cross-session memory — none of which exist in text traces.

### CockpitHAT's Architecture

CockpitHAT replaces positional windows with **dependency-distance thresholds** from interaction DAGs.

**Step 1 — Dependency Graph Construction:** Each trace step becomes a node in a directed acyclic graph. Edges are added when one step actually depends on another through four signals: reference (mentions entity from earlier step), plan (executes a plan proposed earlier), tool (uses tool output from earlier step), control (delegated by earlier step). The dependency distance `dd(vi, vj)` is the shortest path between two nodes — steps far apart in time but linked by a direct dependency edge are dependency-close (dd=1).

**Step 2 — Four-Layer Hierarchical Slicing:** Instead of "show 5 steps before and after," CockpitHAT creates four context layers based on dependency distance:
- **Immediate (dd=1):** Full verbatim content with reasoning chains and tool call/output pairs
- **Local (dd=2):** Compressed to key actions and commitments, routine narration removed
- **Distant (dd=3-4):** 20-word per-step outcome summaries preserving transitions and warnings
- **Global (dd≥5):** Sparse milestones — decision pivots, tool failures, hand-offs, policy escalations

When the dependency graph is sparse and linear, this degenerates gracefully to positional layering. The benefit grows proportionally to dependency richness.

**Step 3 — Embodied Channel Adapter:** For physical systems, a salience scanner flags steps involving safety violations, environmental misgrounding, interaction risk, or cross-domain contamination. Flagged steps get promoted up one layer in the hierarchy. Safety violations are forced into the Immediate layer regardless of dependency distance — hard priority.

**Step 4 — Safety-Uplifted Consensus:** A panel of 4 analysts (one mandatory Safety Analyst + three from a rotating persona pool) independently analyze the layered context. Severity rating follows a maximum-severity prior — the most severe assessment wins, preventing specialist safety alarms from being out-voted by generic analysts. Attribution voting uses an asymmetric uplift where the Safety Analyst's weight increases exponentially with severity level (Ω ranges from 1.0 at ASIL-A to ~2.16 at ASIL-D).

### Results That Matter

On the Who&When benchmark: 77.9% / 37.8% agent-level / step-exact accuracy on Hand-Crafted, 86.5% / 46.0% on Algorithm-Generated — surpassing text-only SOTA by up to 17.6 / 16.7 points. The larger gain on algorithmically-generated traces reflects the strength of dependency-distance slicing when dependency structure is rich.

### What You Should Take From This

1. **If you're only measuring task completion, you have no idea if your multi-agent system actually works.** Process-level evaluation is not optional.
2. **Positional proximity ≠ causal dependency.** Build dependency graphs, not sliding windows.
3. **In safety-critical systems, asymmetric cost matters.** A missed safety failure is not the same cost as a missed benign failure — your aggregation must reflect that.
4. **Attribution is a graph problem.** Once you model it that way, the existing text-only methods look like what they are: crude approximations that happen to work when dependency structure is trivial.

## 2. Master Agent Architecture: Harness, Loop, and Graph

**Source:** Multi-article synthesis (Master Agent Architecture, harness engineering deep dives)
**Core insight:** The three approaches developers fight about — harness engineering, loop engineering, graph engineering — are not competing paradigms. They are three structural layers of a single production system. Miss any one and the agent remains a fragile demo.

### The Three Failure Modes

Most developers use LLM agents like an expensive intern: one prompt, one response, manual checking. The teams that try harder fall into three traps:

1. **Endless retry loops** that burn thousands of dollars without checking if the code compiles
2. **Massive 50-node workflow graphs** drawn before understanding how a single sub-agent works
3. **Repeated prompt rewrites** while ignoring the underlying environment

These fail because builders treat Harness, Loop, and Graph as competing ideas. They're not. They form concentric layers:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              HARNESS LAYER                       │
│  (Environment, Sandboxes, State, Tool Caching)   │
│  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│  │              GRAPH LAYER                  │   │
│  │   (Topology, Parallel Fan-Out, Routing)   │   │
│  │  ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐  │   │
│  │  │            LOOP LAYER               │  │   │
│  │  │  (Evidence Checks, Linters, Retry)  │  │   │
│  │  │  ┌───────────────────────────────┐  │  │   │
│  │  │  │           MODEL              │  │  │   │
│  │  │  └───────────────────────────────┘  │  │   │
│  │  └─────────────────────────────────────┘  │   │
│  └───────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Layer 1: The Harness (Environment and State)

The harness is everything outside the model: code, configuration, sandboxes, git history, memory. A raw LLM cannot execute shell commands, maintain state across turns, inspect a filesystem, or enforce security rules. The harness provides those working conditions.

**The 7-File Production Harness:**

```
.claude/
├── CLAUDE.md                 # Core system instructions and architectural rules
├── settings.json             # Execution timeouts, budget caps, allowed tools
├── hooks/
│   ├── pre_tool_hash.py      # State hashing to prevent redundant file reads
│   └── post_tool_audit.py    # Execution logging and safety policy enforcer
└── memory/
    ├── progress.json         # State tracking across multi-turn sessions
    ├── tool_cache.json       # Zero-latency tool output cache
    └── git_checkpoint.log    # Rollback log for failed sub-agent branches
```

**State Hashing** prevents the most common token waste: re-reading files that haven't changed. SHA-256 hash the file, check against cache, skip the read if unchanged. Simple Python, saves thousands of tokens per session.

**What breaks when the harness is missing:** State lost between turns, file read token leaks, no rollback capability, security violations.

### Layer 2: The Loop (Feedback and Verification)

The core principle: **never let the model decide whether the task is complete. Let deterministic external systems decide.**

A production loop:

```python
while not task_complete:
    result = agent.execute(task)
    evidence = collect_evidence(result)
    if run_tests() and run_linter() and check_constraints():
        task_complete = True
    else:
        diagnose_failure()
        update_context()
        retry()
```

The model generates. The environment generates the evidence. The loop decides whether another attempt is justified.

**Bad evidence:** `Agent output: "All tests should pass now."`
**Good evidence:** `Test runner: 27 passed, 0 failed`

The second is evidence. The first is a sentence produced by a language model. Humanity has spent decades learning not to use sentences as test infrastructure.

**Retry budgets are non-negotiable.** Unbounded loops are dangerous because the model can repeatedly make small changes without increasing success probability. Hard limits on: max token spend, max execution time, max tool calls, max retries, max files modified.

**What breaks when the loop is missing:** Agent claims code works but tests fail, hallucinated completions, no budget controls, infinite retry spirals.

### Layer 3: The Graph (Flow and Concurrency)

The graph defines how work moves through the system. Useful when a task contains independent pieces that can execute simultaneously.

**Five principles:**
1. Scope each node tightly — clear single responsibility
2. Parallelize independent work — if two tasks don't depend on each other, run them concurrently
3. Join only when necessary — don't synchronize unless outputs are actually needed together
4. Route based on evidence — deterministic signals decide which branch executes next
5. Bound cycles — every retry edge has a maximum attempt count

**What breaks when the graph is missing:** Sequential execution bottlenecks, wrong routing, no parallel fan-out.

### The Red-Team Node

A particularly powerful pattern: a dedicated adversarial verification node whose job is not to improve the implementation but to try to break it. Feed it edge cases (null, empty string, million-character payload, invalid input, malformed payload). If the implementation survives, it passes. The red-team node must be independent from the agent that produced the implementation — separation between generation and verification.

### Automated Patch Verification Pipeline

Every patch moves through explicit states: GENERATED → STATIC_CHECK → UNIT_TEST → INTEGRATION_TEST → RED_TEAM → APPROVED. If any stage fails: FAILED → DIAGNOSE → RETRY. The model never decides the final state. The controller does.

### The Numbers

| Metric | Single-Agent "Intern Mode" | Unified 3-Layer Architecture | Delta |
|--------|---------------------------|------------------------------|-------|
| Task execution time | 14.2 min | 2.1 min | 6.7× faster |
| Token spend per PR | $4.80 | $0.94 | 80.4% cost reduction |
| Test pass rate | 42% | 98.6% | 2.3× higher |
| Human escalation | 68% of runs | 4% of runs | 17× reduction |
| Hallucinated file edits | Frequent | Zero | Complete elimination |

## 3. HiGram: Hierarchical Graph Memory

**Source:** HiGram paper (arxiv 2608.05095)
**Core problem:** Existing graph-memory systems for agents pile everything into one flat graph, then drown in junk and rewrite the same facts repeatedly. HiGram introduces coarse-to-fine hierarchy plus a bounded rewrite mechanism that edits only the affected evidence path.

### The Flat Graph Problem

When an agent's memory is a flat knowledge graph, two things go wrong as the graph grows:

1. **Retrieval traverses the entire graph.** Every query potentially touches every node. As the graph grows, retrieval gets slower and noisier — the ratio of relevant to irrelevant evidence degrades.
2. **Updates are local but dependencies are global.** When you patch one node, you might break consistency with ten other nodes that reference it. Independent node-wise updates require repeated rewrites to cover all related changes, leading to unbearable token costs.

The root cause is a **granularity mismatch**: retrieval operates over the continuously expanding full graph, while an answer typically depends on only a small localized evidence path. Updates target single memory units, but their effects propagate along dependency edges. The system should localize the relevant subgraph first, identify the affected evidence path, then jointly revise memory states and dependencies within that bounded region.

### HiGram's Architecture

HiGram introduces a **hierarchical graph memory** organized coarse-to-fine:

**Node hierarchy:**
- **Subject nodes** — entity-level anchors (e.g., "Alice")
- **Object-category nodes** — coarse semantic groupings (e.g., "workplace")
- **Context nodes** — temporal and situational context (e.g., "conversation on Tuesday")
- **MemoryUnits** — the fine-grained facts themselves, each storing: subject, relation, object, object category, transaction time, context, confidence, and a **status field** (active / superseded / outdated / pending)

Upper-level nodes form the routing layer. MemoryUnits store the actual evidence. Superseded and outdated units remain in the graph for revision history but are excluded from active localization — the graph remembers what it used to believe without letting stale beliefs pollute retrieval.

### MicroGraph-Based Path-Level Localization

This is the key architectural innovation. Given a query and an incoming update:

**Step 1 — Temporary MemoryUnit Construction:** Parse the query and update into temporary MemoryUnits following the same schema. These stay outside the graph during localization.

**Step 2 — Anchor Extraction and MicroGraph Selection:** Extract subject and object-category anchors from the temporary units. A **MicroGraph** is a localized subgraph defined by a (subject node, object-category node) pair — it contains all MemoryUnits, context nodes, and dependency edges that fall under both anchors. Select the top-K_g most relevant MicroGraphs (default K_g = 12) by anchor matching.

**Step 3 — Support Subgraph Assembly:** The selected MicroGraphs, their MemoryUnits, context nodes, and dependency edges form a **support subgraph** — a bounded search space much smaller than the full graph.

**Step 4 — Evidence Path Identification:** Within the support subgraph, enumerate up to K_p candidate paths (default K_p = 24) by following dependency edges and structural relations. Each candidate path is scored against the temporary MemoryUnits for attribute matching, dependency consistency, temporal validity, and contextual compatibility. The highest-scoring path becomes the **affected evidence path** — the rewrite target.

### Coordinated Rewriting

Once the evidence path is identified, HiGram performs two coordinated rewrites **within that bounded region only**:

**Intra-unit rewriting:** Match temporary MemoryUnits to units on the evidence path. Update internal states (relation, object, confidence, valid-time) according to new evidence. New memories are committed as active units. Updated units get their status set to active. Previous versions are marked superseded — preserved for history but excluded from future retrieval.

**Inter-unit rewriting:** After intra-unit changes, examine whether dependent MemoryUnits on the path remain valid. If a supporting unit changed state, check whether its downstream dependents are still supported. Unsupported dependents are marked outdated. Dependency edges involving updated units are retained only when the evidence still supports the connection.

**Everything outside the rewrite region is untouched.** This is the critical difference from flat graph approaches that require global search after every update.

### Results That Matter

On **LoCoMo** (long-term conversational QA):
- HiGram achieves best average F1 (50.62), BLEU (46.16), and LLM-Judge score (78.83) with GPT-5.4; and 52.51 / 49.83 / 83.65 with GPT-4o — top on both backbones
- Uses only **7.2% of the tokens consumed by full-context retrieval** (2,032 tokens vs. 28,346 for LoCoMo baseline)
- Strongest advantage on **Temporal questions** (where outdated facts must be resolved) and **Adversarial questions** (where misleading context must be excluded)

On **MemConflict** (conflict-aware memory):
- Best overall Macro-AA (67.84), SEH@3 (81.06), and Support Rank Score (77.31)
- Dominates Static conflicts — AA of 68.75 vs. next best 43.75 (MemOS). The coordinated rewriting prevents overwritten memories from silently staying active
- Best Conditional AA (90.00) — explicit contextual scopes distinguish facts with different applicability conditions

**Ablation evidence:**
- Removing MicroGraph organization increases token cost by **68.6%** and reduces LLM-Judge across all question categories
- Removing the localized support subgraph causes broader quality decline than removing MicroGraphs — path-level localization is more important than coarse-level indexing
- Compared to append-only (no updates) and relation-level (isolated per-unit updates), coordinated rewriting improves average accuracy from 42.89 and 40.76 to **56.77** — a 32% and 39% improvement respectively

### Why This Matters for Your Agent

If your agent's memory is growing over time (and if it's useful, it will), you have two choices:
1. Keep a flat store and accept that retrieval quality degrades and updates break things
2. Build a hierarchy that routes retrieval and scopes updates

The hierarchy costs more to build initially. It costs dramatically less over time. HiGram's specific contribution is the **bounded rewrite**: identify the evidence path first, then edit only that path with both facts and dependencies updated together. This prevents the two failure modes that kill flat graphs — cascading inconsistency and unbounded rewrite cost.

## 4. Stripe Kai: A Production Agent at Enterprise Scale

**Source:** Stripe/LangChain case study on Stripe's Knowledge AI Platform (Kai)
**Core story:** One engineer built a production-grade AI assistant in one week using Deep Agents (LangChain's agent harness). It hit the quarterly adoption target in the first week, grew to 5,000+ users in 4 weeks, and now 83% of Stripe uses it weekly across 60k+ sessions.

### The Architecture

Stripe's stack is a layered architecture where each layer solves a specific class of problems:

1. **Deep Agents (base):** Handles all LLM interaction primitives — request management, agent execution, middleware composition. Solves "all the non-Stripey problems."
2. **Stripe-specific harness:** Integrates Kai into Stripe's security posture, infrastructure, and internal services. Creates an opinionated environment.
3. **Configuration layer:** Teams configure custom Kai agents — specialized instances with different skill sets, behaviors, and personas — without touching the underlying harness.
4. **Kai UI:** The product experience most Stripes interact with, connecting through all three layers.

### Three Middleware That Made It Production-Ready

**Filesystem middleware:** Kai runs in the cloud, not locally. Stripe built a virtual filesystem backed by S3 so agents can read, write, and reference files across turns. Every sandbox call uses a "sync in / sync out" pattern — files materialized before execution, new/modified files synced back after. The agent experiences a coherent, persistent file environment across the full session lifetime. This matters because filesystems work well for LLM context — they turn context into something the model can inspect, update, and organize over time.

**Sandbox middleware:** Code execution (Python for analytics, file processing) runs through a sandboxed environment exposed as a tool, not as the agent's execution environment. The agent runs outside the sandbox and calls into it. This keeps execution boundaries clean and prevents a class of security concerns with LLM-generated code.

**Summarization middleware:** Essential for long-running sessions where accumulated context would degrade performance or hit model limits. Kai leverages tuning knobs for summarization threshold, summarizer model, output size. Most users run sessions intermittently, so avoiding cache misses of large context sizes helps control costs.

### Skills: Navigating 500+ Tools

Kai uses a federated skills model — individual teams own and maintain their own skills. Skills are structured, agent-executable modules that encapsulate how to accomplish a specific task, what tools to load, and how to approach a category of work.

The clever part: with 500+ internal MCP tools and a growing skills library, Kai can't load everything into context. Skills' `allowedTools` lists drive **dynamic tool loading** — a two-pass system where skill selection gates tool context. The LLM picks which skills to load, and those skills determine which tools get loaded. Certain foundational skills are pinned regardless.

**The constraint they hit:** Quality degradation of frontier models with more than 150 skills when combined with the system prompt (1024 character limit on frontmatter). This is an active problem.

### Adoption Numbers

- Quarterly adoption target hit in **one week**
- Grew from 296 → 5,000+ users in ~4 weeks
- 83% of Stripe uses Kai weekly across 60k+ sessions
- Marketing (95%) and GTM teams (87%) have higher adoption than engineering
- Quote: *"My Stripe career is divided into before and after Kai."*

### What's Coming Next

1. **Scaling skill selection:** Building a hybrid system — pure LLM selection works now but won't scale past the skill catalog's current size. A RAG or classifier layer will prefilter before the LLM makes final decisions.
2. **Governance and guardrails:** Security and compliance for enterprise-wide adoption.
3. **Behavioral personalization:** Some teams want "always plan before acting," others want "fast direct answers." The system should figure this out automatically without the user tuning knobs.
4. **Collaborative sessions:** Current model is single-user. Roadmap extends to shared skills, shared artifacts, and collaborative multi-user sessions.

### The Lesson

The one-engineer-one-week story isn't about how easy it is. It's about what happens when the right abstraction layer exists. Deep Agents solved the agent infrastructure problems, so Stripe's engineer could focus entirely on the domain-specific layer. The hard part of building production agents isn't the agent — it's the harness, middleware, and operational infrastructure around it.

## 5. From RAG to Context Graphs: The 9-Step Roadmap

**Source:** Context graph engineering deep dive
**Core claim:** Embeddings retrieve text that mentions the thing you asked about. They cannot retrieve the thing itself, or the chain of relationships that holds the answer. For single-fact lookups that's fine. For anything multi-hop, it's structurally the wrong tool.

### The RAG Ceiling

Ask a chunk retriever "what breaks if Redis goes down" and it hands you the one sentence that contains the word "Redis." That sentence doesn't answer the question. The answer is spread across four documents that never mention each other, and similarity search has no way to walk from one to the next.

This isn't a tuning problem. This is a structural limitation. The fix: stop retrieving passages and start retrieving a graph — entities as nodes, relationships as edges, answers as paths.

### Tier I — Extract

**Step 01: Start from the question RAG can't answer.** Don't begin by building a graph. Write down the query your retriever fails. That query defines the entities and relations the graph needs. The failing queries have a shape: they're multi-hop. "What depends on the thing that depends on the thing that Redis backs." If your hardest queries are single-hop lookups, you don't need a graph.

**Step 02: Pull entities and relations, not embeddings.** A context graph reads each chunk and extracts triples: subject → relation → object. In production the extractor is an LLM call. The `chunk_id` on every triple is not an afterthought — it's what makes the eventual answer citable. RAG throws this away the moment it concatenates chunks into a prompt.

**Step 03: Canonicalize, or the graph fractures.** "The Auth Service," "auth service," and "AuthService" are one entity. If the graph treats them as three, every edge lands on a different node and paths never connect. Lowercase, strip articles, collapse whitespace, apply an alias map. This is entity resolution in miniature, and getting it wrong is the single most common reason homegrown GraphRAG returns nothing useful.

### Tier II — Connect

**Step 04: Build the edge list.** Forward and reverse adjacency maps, because you'll traverse in both directions: forward for "what does X depend on," reverse for "what depends on X." Storing both directions doubles memory and removes an entire class of "I can only search one way" bugs.

**Step 05: Weight edges by corroboration.** If two different chunks both say the auth service depends on the token cache, that edge is weight 2, stronger than a single-source assertion. When two paths could answer a question, the one built from corroborated edges is the one to trust.

**Step 06: Attach provenance to every edge.** Every edge carries the set of chunks it came from. This is what makes a context graph answerable rather than merely suggestive — when the graph returns a path, it returns the receipt for each hop. A graph that cannot cite its edges is a pile of assertions with better packaging.

### Tier III — Traverse

**Step 07: Retrieve by k-hop neighborhood.** Context for a question is not the top-k most similar chunks. It's the subgraph within k hops of the entities the question names. BFS from the starting entity, collect everything within k hops.

**Step 08: Rank paths, not passages.** The multi-hop question is answered by reverse reachability. If Redis breaks, walk the reverse edges. The result is a ranked list of **paths**, each a causal chain:

```
token cache via redis <- token cache
auth service via redis <- token cache <- auth service
login flow via redis <- token cache <- auth service <- login flow
survives: billing, platform team, postgresql, user database
```

Three things break, four survive, and the graph can name both sets. A chunk retriever can't produce the "survives" list at all — nothing in the corpus states it. It's only visible in the structure.

**Step 09: Assemble the subgraph with citations.** Hand the model a subgraph, not a wall of text. Nodes on the answer path, edges connecting them, chunk ID behind each edge. The model's job shrinks from "find the answer in this pile" to "read this chain and phrase it." You've moved the hard part — connecting scattered facts — out of the prompt and into inspectable structure.

### Three Gates, Three Tiers

Notice where the gates sit: canonicalization ends Tier I, provenance ends Tier II, the ranked path ends Tier III. A context graph with no citation is RAG with extra steps.

### When NOT to Build a Graph

If your hardest questions are single-hop lookups, embed your chunks and move on. The test is Step 1: are your queries lookups or chains? If they're lookups, RAG is simpler, cheaper, and correct. If they're chains, you're already asking the graph a question — you just haven't built it yet.

If you pass the test: build small. One relation type. One canonical entity table. One traversal. Get "what depends on X" returning a cited path before you add a second edge type.

**The point was never better search over text. It was retrieving structure instead of prose.**

## 6. Filesystem-Based Memory for LLM Agents

**Source:** UIUC / UCSD / Adobe — arxiv 2607.26637
**Core question:** Deployed agents already store memory as markdown files in a directory tree. Does this actually work as memory scales? Nobody had tested it.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Research built exotic memory systems — paged context (MemGPT), fact stores (Mem0), temporal knowledge graphs (Zep), self-linking note networks (A-Mem), embedding-organized trees. Every one behind its own custom interface.

Meanwhile, production converged on something plain: the filesystem. Anthropic's memory tool exposes memory as a directory of markdown files behind six generic file operations. Claude Code maintains agent-written notes in an indexed memory folder. Skills ship as repository markdown. The filesystem won because it's inspectable, portable, and operated with the file tools agents already know. It's natively hierarchical — folders form a taxonomy whose names are labels.

**The problem:** Nobody tested the two assumptions this default rests on. First, that an agent can keep a growing store organized as memories accumulate, conflict, and go stale. Second, that organization actually pays for itself in downstream quality or cost.

### The Core Mechanism

The paper formalizes filesystem memory as three roles around a single store:

1. **Management agent** — integrates incoming content into the store. Can create, rewrite, merge, split, move, or delete anything. Organization is part of its job, not a side effect.
2. **Search agent** — answers queries over a fixed (read-only) store with cited sources. It navigates the store through file tools, not through a search engine that would hide the organizational structure.
3. **Execution agent** — attempts tasks given retrieved skills, supplies trajectories that become the chunks the management agent integrates. This is only present in the skill/embodied setting.

The **taxonomy contract** — five principles for what a well-organized memory store looks like:
- **P1 Sibling distinction:** Items under one parent are distinguishable by name alone
- **P2 Sibling relatedness:** Things that share a parent are related enough that sharing is natural
- **P3 Parent-child coverage:** Everything under a parent falls within what its name declares; descending narrows search without losing the sought fact
- **P4 Tree-wide proximity:** The more related two pieces of content, the nearer they sit in the tree
- **P5 Structural economy:** Depth added only where it improves routing to a fact — a level that doesn't help routing is overhead

They test six memory variants across four benchmarks: no store, chunk retrieval (standard RAG), verbatim dump (session files, zero curation), foldered sessions (taxonomy applied to session files), reorganized store (with and without a "keep every fact" preservation rule), and a fully agent-curated store built from scratch.

### What Organization Actually Buys

The headline result splits cleanly:

**Organization reliably buys search economy.** Organized stores roughly halve retrieval cost when material is large. The search agent navigates a well-organized taxonomy faster, with fewer file reads and fewer tokens consumed.

**Organization does NOT reliably buy better answers.** No agent they measured converts organization itself into better answers. The search agent's own model strength pays directly for answer quality. The management agent's strength buys organizational style — a cleaner store — but that cleaner store doesn't produce measurably better answers in most settings.

**The degenerate behavior:** When a management agent reorganizes freely, it silently condenses — the store shrinks, the model drops detail as it rewrites. Adding one "keep every fact" preservation rule counteracts this. Without it, reorganization can degrade the store below the no-memory baseline. This is exactly the failure mode Zhang et al. (2026) documented: continuously rewriting a memory bank with an LLM can destroy it.

**The harness is not neutral.** Changing the tool set alone reshapes the store as strongly as swapping the model. Adding BM25 search or replacing file tools with a bash shell changes both the store's structure and its effectiveness. The tool harness is a lever over memory organization, not a passive wrapper.

**The sustainability finding:** Within their horizons, stores only become more useful as they grow, and accumulated experience substitutes for execution-agent capability. Store health holds — conversational stores create files early and afterwards only edit them. But organizational adherence erodes for all but the strongest management agent.

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** The filesystem won for the same reason SQL won — it's boring, inspectable, and composed with existing tools. If you're building exotic memory representations, you need evidence they beat organized markdown files, and this paper says that bar is higher than you think.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** Memory management is a capability that degrades as the store grows. The agent gets worse at maintaining its own memory the more memory it has. Only the strongest models hold organizational contracts under scale. This means your memory system needs explicit preservation rules ("keep every fact," "never condense without permission") — or periodic external cleanup (Anthropic's "Dreams" rebuilds). The agent will compress and corrupt its own memory if left unsupervised.

3. **Essential knowledge.** If you're building any long-running agent with persistent memory, this paper tells you what works (organized markdown files, preservation rules, strong search agents) and what doesn't (assuming organization improves answers, trusting agents to maintain their own stores indefinitely). Skip this and you'll waste months building something worse than a filesystem.

## 7. Argus: General-Purpose Agentic Runtime for Long-Horizon Reasoning

**Source:** Microsoft / SJTU — Argus agentic runtime
**Core problem:** Long-horizon agent tasks fail not because the model is dumb but because the harness around it doesn't manage state, track progress, or recover from failures well enough.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Before Argus, agent runtimes either handled short tasks (single-turn tool calls, basic ReAct loops) or were purpose-built for specific domains (SWE-Agent for code, WebArena agents for browser). None provided a general-purpose runtime for tasks that span dozens or hundreds of steps across heterogeneous environments — where the agent needs to maintain beliefs about the world, track which sub-goals have been accomplished, and decide when to retry versus abandon.

### The Core Mechanism

Argus introduces a **runtime harness** designed for long-horizon execution. The key architectural contributions:

1. **Structured state management:** Instead of dumping the entire interaction history into the prompt, Argus maintains structured representations of the agent's current beliefs, progress, and available actions. This prevents the context window from becoming a garbage dump of stale observations.

2. **Sub-goal tracking:** The agent explicitly commits to sub-goals and tracks their completion status. This prevents the common failure mode where an agent re-attempts something it already accomplished, or forgets that a prerequisite step needs to happen first.

3. **Recovery and retry logic:** When an action fails, Argus distinguishes between transient failures (retry the same action) and structural failures (the approach is wrong, backtrack to a different strategy). This is the difference between a robust system and an infinite retry loop.

4. **Environment abstraction:** Argus provides a uniform interface regardless of whether the agent is operating on a filesystem, a web browser, a database, or a simulated environment. The policy logic doesn't change when the environment changes.

### What This Creates for Your System

Argus demonstrates that the gap between "model can solve this in principle" and "agent reliably solves this in practice" is almost entirely a harness engineering problem. The same model, with the same weights, produces dramatically different success rates depending on how state is managed, how progress is tracked, and how failures are handled.

The production lesson: before you fine-tune a model or switch to a more expensive one, instrument your harness. Most agent failures are harness failures wearing model-failure costumes.

### The Honest Take

1. **What engineers miss:** The runtime harness is the product. The model is a component. Most teams spend 90% of their effort on prompt engineering and 10% on harness engineering. The ratio should be reversed.

2. **Non-obvious implication:** Argus shows that structured state is cheaper than raw context. Maintaining a progress tracker, a belief tracker, and a sub-goal tracker costs a few hundred tokens. Stuffing the entire interaction history costs thousands. The structured approach is both cheaper and more effective.

3. **Useful context.** Not essential reading for every engineer, but important if you're building agents that need to operate over more than ~10 steps. The core ideas (structured state, sub-goal tracking, recovery logic) are more important than the specific Argus implementation.

## 8. EvoHarness-RL: Learning Self-Evolving Runtime Harness

**Source:** UIUC / Meta — arxiv, EvoHarness-RL
**Core insight:** Instead of hand-engineering when an agent should use its harness tools (check beliefs, recall experience, track progress), train the agent to learn when to use them through reinforcement learning.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Every harness system before this treated the harness as a static scaffold. The human decides: "always check your notes before acting," "always commit your sub-goal before exploring." The agent follows these rules whether or not they're useful in the current situation. The result: agents that use their harness too much early (wasting steps on unnecessary bookkeeping) or not enough late (forgetting useful experience).

### The Core Mechanism

EvoHarness-RL introduces a **trainable coordination layer** between the agent and its harness. The harness is organized into three workspaces:

- **Belief (B):** What the agent thinks is true about the current environment
- **Progress (P):** Which sub-goals have been committed and completed
- **Experience (E):** Cross-episode skills, lessons, and search priorities

The agent has four harness actions: `commit` (register a sub-goal), `track` (query object state), `recall` (retrieve experience), and `note` (record a discovery). These sit alongside the normal environment actions.

**The training recipe is two-stage:**

1. **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning):** A teacher model (Claude Opus) generates demonstration trajectories showing how to use the BPE harness. This produces 87 successful trajectories from 500 ALFWorld games — the SFT checkpoint and an initial skill bank.

2. **Cost-aware GRPO:** Group Relative Policy Optimization with a reward that includes task success MINUS a cost penalty for each harness action. This trains the policy to use the harness selectively — only when the expected benefit exceeds the step cost.

### Harness Annealing: The Key Dynamic

The most interesting finding: **harness usage drops during training even as success rises.** Early in RL training, the agent uses the harness heavily — checking beliefs, recalling experience, tracking everything. As training progresses, usage drops and stabilizes near one call per episode. The policy has *internalized* the routine scaffolded behaviors and now only uses the harness when the expected benefit genuinely exceeds its cost.

Different harness actions anneal at different rates:
- `recall` (retrieving past experience) is the most persistent — it remains useful even after many behaviors are internalized
- `commit` and `note` rapidly decay toward zero — the agent stops externalizing plans and insights once stable strategies emerge
- `track` follows an intermediate pattern — useful for early disambiguation, then decreases

### Experience Store Evolution

The skill bank evolves across episodes through accumulation, consolidation, and forgetting:
- Early: rapid expansion with general strategies, task-specific procedures, common mistakes, search priorities
- Later: selective growth as redundant entries are merged, rarely-used skills are evicted (LFU), and frequently-recalled knowledge is preserved
- Final: compact yet diverse — a task-adaptive substrate, not an append-only log

The consolidation model (Claude Opus) runs outside the rollout loop at epoch boundaries, updating the experience store from buffered notes. This keeps the skill bank stable within a rollout batch while allowing cross-episode evolution.

### Results

On ALFWorld: EvoHarness-RL achieves higher reward than standard GRPO throughout training. Its reward increases faster and reaches a higher plateau. The reduction in harness calls is NOT policy collapse — it's selective efficiency. The agent learned when external state is worth its cost.

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** Harness use is not free. Every time you force an agent to "always check its notes" or "always plan before acting," you're spending tokens that might be better used on another attempt. The right amount of scaffolding is learned, not prescribed.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** The optimal harness usage is environment-dependent. In household tasks, Experience (cross-episode priors) is most valuable. In coding tasks, Progress (tracking sub-tasks, test status) would be more important. In vision tasks, Belief (maintaining scene graphs) would dominate. You cannot design one fixed harness protocol for all environments.

3. **Useful context.** Not essential for most engineers today (you're unlikely to RL-train your own harness coordination). But the principle — that harness interaction should be learned and budget-aware, not scripted — is directionally important. It tells you where the field is heading: harnesses that adapt their behavior based on the task and the agent's capability, rather than fixed rules.

## 9. Model or Harness? An Interaction-Centric Failure Taxonomy

**Source:** Scale AI — arxiv 2607.28802
**Core problem:** When an agent fails, you need to know whether to fix the model or the harness. Existing failure taxonomies don't tell you.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Previous agent failure taxonomies were either benchmark-specific (useful only for one eval) or module-specific (memory errors, planning errors, action errors — categories that describe where the failure manifests but not where it originates). The same visible failure — the agent deleted 200 emails — could be caused by context compaction dropping the user's "don't act" instruction (harness fix) or by the model ignoring an instruction that was present (model fix). The repair is completely different, but existing taxonomies assign the same label.

### The Core Mechanism

The taxonomy is built on two concepts:

1. **Edge:** The interaction between two components. Written as `COMP1 — COMP2`. Nine components are defined: Model, Owner, Grader, Third party, Context, Memory, Tool, Local Environment, External Environment. In multi-agent settings, the other model enters as a peer or subagent.

2. **Fault side:** Which component at the interaction boundary is responsible. Written as `fault: SIDE`.

A failure is localized as: `COMP1 — COMP2 · fault: SIDE`

Example: A tool returns an error, and the model ignores it. This is `TOOL — MODEL · fault: MODEL` (the tool did its job; the model failed to handle the response). Versus: a tool wrapper suppresses an error before the model sees it. This is `TOOL — MODEL · fault: TOOL` (the model never had a chance).

### The 41 Failure Modes

Organized across three families:

**User Family (12 modes):**
- MODEL — OWNER: Over-initiative, Under-initiative, Satisficing, Instruction-Following Failure, Reasoning Failure, Unauthorized Irreversible Action, Sycophancy, Domain Knowledge Deficit, Value Misalignment, Instruction–Grader Mismatch (owner-side)
- MODEL — GRADER: Specification Gaming, Evaluation Awareness
- MODEL — THIRD PARTY: Indirect Prompt Injection, Contextual Sycophancy

**Harness Family (21 modes):**
- MODEL — CONTEXT: State Tracking Failure, Goal Drift, Context Rationale Erosion
- MODEL — MEMORY: 8 modes covering Missed Write, State Staleness, Overgeneralization, Memory Rationale Erosion, Pollution, Redundancy, Missed Read, Memory Following Failure
- MODEL — TOOL: 7 modes from Incorrect Selection to Mistranslation
- MODEL — MODEL (peer/subagent): Delegation Failure, Communication Failure

**Environment Family (5 modes):**
- MODEL — EXTERNAL: Recovery Failure, Service Failure, Stale State Delivery
- MODEL — LOCAL: Observation Failure, Recovery Failure

**The attribution rule:** Start from the observed system-level failure and trace backward. Label the earliest failure from which execution does not recover. This prevents labeling downstream symptoms — you find the root cause.

### Validation: Agent-as-a-Judge

Four frontier models (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.6/4.7/4.8) independently applied the taxonomy to 40 worked examples. The strongest judge (GPT-5.5) reached Cohen's κ = 0.76 against human labels. The judges agree with each other about as strongly as they agree with humans — highest pairwise κ = 0.84 between Opus 4.6 and 4.8. This means the taxonomy captures shared structure, not annotator idiosyncrasy.

Selective voting improves precision: unanimity among all four judges yields 0.96 category precision at 68% coverage.

### What This Creates for Your System

**The repair-assignment problem is now solvable.** When your agent fails, this taxonomy tells you: fix the model (post-training, prompting), fix the harness (context management, tool integration, memory), or fix the environment (API reliability, sandbox config). Without this, teams waste weeks "improving the prompt" when the real problem is that context compaction dropped a critical instruction.

**36 of 41 modes are model-side.** This isn't because harnesses work well — it's because the attribution rule assigns fault to the model whenever a more capable model could have avoided the failure. The 5 non-model failures are the ones model improvement alone cannot fix.

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** "The agent failed" is not a useful diagnosis. "The agent failed at the TOOL — MODEL interaction because it ignored a tool error (fault: MODEL)" is actionable. "The agent failed at the CONTEXT — MODEL interaction because compaction dropped the user instruction (fault: CONTEXT)" tells you to fix your summarization, not retrain the model.

2. **Non-obvious implication:** Satisficing (cutting scope to finish sooner) is cataloged as a model-side failure mode against the owner. Your agent declaring "I've completed the task" when half the requirements are unmet is a named, studied failure mode with a specific intervention target: model behavior, not harness config.

3. **Essential knowledge.** If you debug agents for a living, this is your diagnostic handbook. The 41-mode taxonomy with edge + fault-side localization is the most actionable failure classification framework published. Memorize the families, apply the attribution rule. It will save you weeks of misdiagnosed debugging.

## 10. Zero-Mem: Zero-Token Memory Operations

**Source:** Hong Kong PolyU / SWUFE — arxiv 2607.29377
**Core insight:** Every existing memory system uses LLM calls to manage memory — summarizing, reflecting, constructing abstractions. Zero-Mem proves you can eliminate all LLM calls from memory operations. Only the final question-answering step needs a model.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Memory systems for LLM agents follow two paradigms, both with structural problems:

1. **Generative memory** (Mem0, A-Mem, MemoryOS, CompassMem): Use LLM calls to summarize, compress, link, and abstract interaction histories into structured memory representations. The problem: these LLM calls are expensive, recurring, and lossy — omitted details, merged subjects, and blurred temporal updates weaken traceability to the original interaction. When the generated abstraction mediates later retrieval, the original evidence is one or more abstraction layers away.

2. **Raw retrieval** (flat chunk indexes): Retain the complete history and retrieve directly with lexical or dense similarity. The problem: flat retrieval confuses semantically similar traces from different users, sessions, or temporal states. When supporting evidence is distributed across multiple interactions, similarity search cannot walk between them.

### The Core Mechanism

Zero-Mem retains original interaction traces as the authoritative source and builds two complementary, non-generative views over them:

**View 1 — Entity-Context Graph:** Uses a non-generative NER model (spaCy) to extract entities from each trace unit. Constructs a graph where entity nodes are connected to context nodes via co-occurrence edges, and adjacent context units are connected via adjacency edges. No LLM call needed — pure NER + graph construction.

**View 2 — Temporal Hierarchy:** Organizes traces at multiple granularities: turns → windows → episodes → local spans. Episodes group adjacent windows into coherent event regions by semantic continuity. All units inherit provenance from raw traces. No LLM call needed — rule-based segmentation.

**Query-time routing:** For each query, a lightweight profile determines which view gets priority. Relational queries (about connections between entities) favor the graph view. Local queries (about what happened in a specific context) favor the temporal hierarchy. Both views always execute — routing only controls relative weights in fusion.

**Evidence retrieval:**
- **Graph view:** Uses personalized PageRank. Matches query entities to graph entities, propagates activation through co-occurrence, runs PageRank to distribute relevance scores across the graph. Refines with exact lexical matches.
- **Hierarchical view:** Coarse-to-fine search: episodes → windows → turns → local spans. Narrows down through semantic relevance and structural compatibility.

**Evidence closure:** After fusion, Zero-Mem supplements the main evidence with graph bridges (relational connections) and local neighbors (surrounding turns) — bounded expansion that adds supporting context without explosion.

**Deterministic calibration:** Before the LLM sees the evidence, rule-based checks discard conflicting evidence, verify type compatibility, and normalize formats. After the LLM produces an answer, rule-based checks verify evidence support, answer type, and format. No LLM calls.

### Results That Matter

On LoCoMo (the standard long-memory benchmark): Zero-Mem achieves 59.15 average F1 with GPT-4o-mini reader — beating GAM (53.75), CompassMem (52.18), SimpleMem (48.02), HippoRAG (47.92), and all other baselines. With Qwen2.5-14B reader: comparable performance.

**The efficiency story:** Zero-Mem reduces memory-operation time cost by 57.6% relative to the fastest compared baseline. With the same final-QA reader and equivalent context budget, all the computational savings come from eliminating LLM calls from memory operations.

### What This Creates for Your System

The cost structure of agent memory changes fundamentally. Today, every Mem0 `add`, every A-Mem `link`, every MemoryOS `update` is an LLM call. Over thousands of interactions, those calls dominate cost. Zero-Mem shows these calls are unnecessary — structured views over raw traces plus deterministic calibration achieves competitive or superior performance.

The tradeoff: encoder computation (NER, embeddings) replaces LLM generation. Encoders are 10-100x cheaper per token than decoders. You're trading expensive generation for cheap encoding.

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** The LLM calls in your memory pipeline are not adding signal proportional to their cost. They're adding abstractions that sometimes help, often lose detail, and always cost money. Zero-Mem shows the abstraction layer can be replaced with structure — graphs and hierarchies that are cheaper, faster, and preserve provenance.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** Provenance matters more than abstraction. When your memory system summarizes a fact, it loses the citation. When your memory system generates a memory card, the original evidence is gone. Zero-Mem's killer feature isn't speed — it's that every retrieved piece of evidence traces directly to an original interaction with a source identifier. This makes answers citable and debuggable, which generated memories are not.

3. **Useful context, potentially essential.** If your agent has long-running memory that grows over time and you're paying for LLM calls to manage that memory, Zero-Mem is the existence proof that you don't have to. The specific architecture may not be what you deploy, but the principle — raw traces + structure + deterministic calibration beats LLM-generated abstractions — is likely to reshape memory system design. Worth understanding deeply.

## 11. Sample More, Reflect Less: Repeated Sampling Beats Self-Refine

**Source:** Stony Brook — arxiv 2607.28576
**Core claim:** When you control for total token budget, repeated sampling with majority vote beats Self-Refine, Reflexion, and every other self-inspection method across all model sizes tested (1.5B to 7B). Spending tokens to reconsider an answer is a worse use of them than spending the same tokens on another attempt.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

A large family of methods promises to make language models reason better without retraining: Self-Refine (critique and rewrite your answer), Reflexion (reflect on mistakes and retry), Plan-and-Solve, Tree of Thoughts, multi-agent debate. They all report gains over single chain-of-thought.

The problem nobody controlled for: **every one of these methods generates far more tokens than a single chain of thought.** Self-Refine with three rounds of criticism is seven LLM calls instead of one. A debate between three model copies over two rounds might be 2,500 tokens instead of 300. The methods aren't the only thing that changed — the compute budget changed by 5-10x.

Wang et al. (2024) made this argument and concluded that simple sampling often wins when budgets are matched. But their evidence had no confidence intervals, no significance tests, and only 100 questions per dataset. Whether the conclusion survives proper statistical inference was unknown.

### The Core Mechanism

The paper does exactly one thing with rigorous statistical methodology:

1. **Measure each method's actual token cost** — total tokens generated across all calls, including critiques, reflections, debate turns, verification steps. These are tokens paid for even if they never appear in the final answer.

2. **Compare against self-consistency at that method's own cost.** Self-consistency (sample N chains, take majority vote) gives a curve of accuracy vs. cost. The method is worth its cost only if it lands above this curve. They build this curve efficiently from a pool of 16 independent chains per question.

3. **Report every comparison with paired bootstrap confidence intervals and Holm-corrected p-values.** 36 comparisons total, corrected for multiple testing.

### Results That Matter

**No method beats repeated sampling at equal cost in any of the 36 settings.**

Ten comparisons are **reliably worse** — all involving methods where the model inspects its own output. Two survive Holm correction. All 18 comparisons involving self-inspection come out negative.

**The interesting scaling behavior:**
- **Best-of-N (model picks the best of 8 samples) vs. Majority Vote:** Counting wins by 8.0 and 11.3 points at 1.5B, but only 2.0 and 1.3 points at 7B (indistinguishable from zero). The model's ability to judge its own work scales with size, but still doesn't beat the baseline at 7B.
- **Self-Refine and forced Reflexion:** Remain significantly below the equal-cost baseline at 7B, by 3.6 to 10.1 points. **Rewriting doesn't recover at any tested size.**

**The Reflexion bug:** On the smallest model (1.5B), Reflexion **never triggered its own retry.** It judged itself correct on every question, silently became a single chain of thought, and scored well because it had become cheap. A method that decides for itself when to act can stop acting without any outward sign.

### What This Creates for Your System

The mental model is brutal and simple: **self-evaluation is where these methods lose, not lack of extra computation.** With the sampled candidates held fixed (identical samples, identical tokens), a model-chosen answer is worse than a counted one. The model is using tokens on inspection that would produce more value as another independent attempt.

**Production implications:**
- If you're using Self-Refine or critique loops in your pipeline, measure the total token cost and compare against running that many independent samples with majority vote. The evidence says the simpler approach wins.
- If you're using Best-of-N with a model-based selector, consider replacing the selection step with majority vote. Below 7B, the model's selection actively hurts.
- Budget-awareness is key: the right comparison is never "did the method beat a single attempt?" It's "did the method beat what I could have done with those same tokens spent differently?"

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** Every "reasoning enhancement" method has an implicit token cost that nobody reports. When you account for that cost, the simple baseline (sample more, majority vote) usually wins. The method's idea is not what helped — the extra compute is what helped, and there's a cheaper way to spend it.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** The Reflexion failure mode — a method that decides when to act and silently stops acting — is a general risk with any self-gated system. Your retry mechanism might be doing nothing without you knowing. Monitor whether your self-improvement loops actually trigger, not just whether the final accuracy is acceptable.

3. **Essential knowledge.** If you're spending tokens on any form of self-critique, self-reflection, or model-based selection in your pipeline, this paper is directly actionable. It doesn't say these methods can never work (they might at frontier scale). It says the burden of proof is on the method, and the proof requires cost-controlled comparison. Most published gains disappear under that standard.

## 12. Harness-R1: Learning to Edit Executable Runtime Harnesses

**Source:** SJTU / Xiaohongshu — arxiv 2608.02276
**Core insight:** Instead of prompting a strong model to edit your agent's harness, train a dedicated 9B harness engineer through reinforcement learning so that its edits are optimized for realized task success.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Agent harnesses — the code that assembles context, mediates tools, validates actions, and recovers from failures — are usually hand-crafted or edited by prompting frontier models. Both approaches are unreliable:

- **Hand-crafted:** Doesn't adapt to the agent's actual failure patterns
- **Prompted frontier models:** Gains are unstable and limited. A fixed Self-Refine rule lowers reward on all three tested benchmarks. Frontier model edits sometimes reduce WebShop reward — they can make things worse.

The fundamental issue: editing a production runtime is hard because the editable surface spans interdependent execution stages. Unrestricted code edits can break existing interfaces or introduce changes unrelated to task success. Text form and static rules cannot determine patch quality — only the target agent's behavior after applying the patch reveals whether the edit helped.

### The Core Mechanism

Harness-R1 separates the **harness engineer** (trainable) from the **target agent** (frozen).

**The loop:**
1. Run frozen target agent on a batch of tasks → collect failure trajectories
2. Compact failures into a failure packet (task constraints, selected action-observation excerpts, outcomes)
3. Harness engineer reads the failure packet and generates an **executable overlay** — runtime patches at four lifecycle points
4. Validate the overlay (parse, check for syntax/runtime errors)
5. Install the overlay, rerun the **same frozen target** on the **same tasks**
6. Reward = performance change (success after patch minus success before patch)
7. Update only the engineer weights via GRPO

**The four lifecycle points the engineer can edit:**

| Hook | What It Does | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Episode Init | Sets up starting context, episode state, skills | Load task-relevant tool hints |
| Pre-Decision | Augments context with guidance before agent decides | "Before buying, verify the price…" |
| Pre-Action | Runtime guardrail: can rewrite or veto an action before it reaches environment | "Do not buy this item: its price exceeds budget" |
| Post-Feedback | Inspects observation, triggers recovery when trajectory stalls | "The current action is looping. Change strategy." |

**Training:**
- **Cold-start SFT:** GPT-5.5 teacher proposes ~1,000 executable editing examples from failure packets. Filter to keep only executable, complete, non-regressive responses. Train the 9B engineer on these.
- **Online GRPO:** Sample 8 candidate patches per failure packet. Validate each, install valid patches, rerun frozen target, compute reward. Normalize rewards within the group, update engineer via clipped surrogate objective.

### Results

**Vanilla target (Qwen3.5-9B):** Harness-R1 raises success from 44.3% to 53.6% (+9.3 points). Beats all frontier model editors — strongest frontier (GLM-5.2) only reaches 48.8%.

**After direct target-agent fine-tuning:** Agent SFT raises success to 59.2%. A target-specific Harness-R1 engineer raises it further to 64.2% (+5.0 points). **Harness optimization and model training are complementary, not substitutes.**

**Cross-model transfer:** Applied to 21 unseen target models (different families: Llama, Gemma, Qwen), the learned editing policy improves 56 of 63 target-benchmark combinations. Average gain: 7.06 points. Every target-level average is positive. A single training recipe transfers across model families and scales.

**Lifecycle ablation:** Pre-action mediation and post-feedback recovery are the two most important hooks (removing them costs 3.9 and 3.3 points respectively). Episode-start and pre-decision matter less (0.9 and 0.6 points).

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** The harness is not a wrapper — it's half the agent's intelligence. Identical model weights produce substantially different capabilities under different harnesses. If you haven't optimized your harness, you're leaving most of your performance budget on the table.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** Harness editing is a learnable skill. You can train a dedicated model to improve harnesses from failure evidence, and this trained editor consistently outperforms prompted frontier models. The editing quality comes from outcome-grounded training (learning what actually helps) not from model scale (having a bigger model propose edits).

3. **Useful context, directionally important.** Most teams won't train their own harness engineer tomorrow, but the principle changes how you should think about agent improvement. Don't just retrain the actor — also improve the runtime around it. The two gains stack. This is the "co-evolution" thesis: harness engineer and target agent improve together.

## 13. DataSpace: Benchmarking Data Agents for Verifiable Analytics

**Source:** HKUST(GZ) / Tsinghua — arxiv 2608.03451
**Core problem:** Existing data agent benchmarks test one thing at a time — structured querying, retrieval, or open-ended analysis. Real analytical work requires discovering evidence scattered across databases, CSVs, JSONs, PDFs, markdown docs, and videos, all in one workspace.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Benchmarks for data agents were either structured-only (text-to-SQL) or retrieval-only (document QA). None required agents to:
- Cross heterogeneous file formats in a single query
- Produce complete tabular results (not just text answers)
- Have results deterministically verified (header-invariant column alignment, type-aware normalization, order-aware row comparison)

### The Core Mechanism

DataSpace is a benchmark with 410 cross-language tasks and 7,439 artifacts totaling 15.01 GB across CSV, JSON, SQLite, Markdown, PDF, and **video**. Each agent receives only a question and a workspace, and must return a complete tabular result.

**The construction pipeline (DataSpace-Builder):**
1. Cross-language transformation (supports Chinese and English)
2. Constraint-aware relational sampling — ensures tasks have determinate answers
3. Modality routing and artifact rendering — decides which evidence goes into which format
4. Human review and task repair by 11 domain experts

**The evaluator** is deterministic: header-invariant column alignment, type- and precision-aware normalization, and order-aware row comparison. No judge model — the answer is either right or wrong.

### Results

**Best accuracy: 66.34%.** The benchmark is unsaturated.

**Harness choice creates a 15.36-point spread** with the same backbone model. The harness matters more than minor model differences.

**Multimodal evidence integration and joins consistently reduce accuracy** across all six backbones. The moment you need to extract evidence from a video and join it with data from a SQLite database, even frontier models struggle badly.

### The Honest Take

1. **What engineers miss:** Your data agent probably works great on clean SQL tasks. Give it a workspace where the answer requires combining a PDF table with a video timestamp and a JSON config, and accuracy drops catastrophically. Heterogeneous evidence integration is the real bottleneck.

2. **Non-obvious implication:** Harness architecture matters more than model selection for data analytics tasks. A 15-point spread from harness choice, with the model fixed, means you should prototype harness designs before comparing models.

3. **Useful context.** Important if you're building data analytics agents. The benchmark is well-constructed and the deterministic evaluation is a breath of fresh air. Not essential for general agent engineering, but essential if your agents need to produce verifiable tabular outputs from heterogeneous sources.

## 14. Same Task, Different Work: Prompt-Induced Waste in Coding Agents

**Source:** PointFive — arxiv 2608.01347
**Core discovery:** Two prompts can request the same code change and produce the same correct patch, yet cause a coding agent to perform radically different amounts of work. The prompt doesn't just describe the objective — it prescribes the work.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Prompt engineering for agents was treated as a text-compression problem: make the prompt shorter, clearer, more precise. Nobody measured what happens when the prompt tells the agent HOW to work, not just WHAT to produce.

### The Core Mechanism

Preregistered benchmark: 4,644 valid runs, 24 deterministic tasks, six open-weight reasoning models, two harnesses (PI.DEV and Claude Code), plus replication studies with Kimi-K3 and Claude Sonnet 5.

**Five prompt families tested:**

| Family | Effect | Success Gain |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| **Multiple approaches** | 2.4-7.4× reasoning on all six holdout models. ~3 elaborated but discarded solution branches, exactly 1 implemented | None |
| **Deep thinking** | 1.6-2.2× reasoning; ~2.2× recorded text with no new functional units | None |
| **Maximum certainty** | More re-verification, repeated tests, post-completion activity | None |
| **Verbose repetition** | ~1.0× reasoning despite a much longer prompt | None |
| **Bounded efficiency** | At or below baseline reasoning, with diagnosis and validation preserved | Preserved |

### The Two Waste Mechanisms

**1. Branch Tournaments (Token-Borne):**
"Multiple approaches" does exactly what it says — the model considers 3.5 additional approaches on average, elaborates ~3 that are never used, implements exactly 1. The instruction buys an internal tournament whose losing branches are discarded. First discarded branch raises median cost by ~1.9×, while tool calls stay nearly flat (7 to 8). The agent is elaborating alternatives in reasoning text and then abandoning them.

**2. Verification Loops (Tool-Borne):**
"Maximum certainty" follows a different path. It adds re-verification of established facts, repeated tests, and activity after the last edit. Every additional tool result re-enters context, inducing another turn. At the highest observed level: **18× the clean-run median cost, 2.5× tool calls, 3× wall-clock, zero success gradient.**

### Harness Amplification

The harness amplifies both mechanisms. In matched settings:
- Claude Code carries a **12-15× larger static prefix**
- Uses **2-7× more turns**
- Costs **5-30× more per success** than PI.DEV

The harness prefix size, turn discipline, and tool policy determine how often the consequences of a wasteful instruction get replayed.

### The Bounded-Efficiency Instruction

The negative control is the most useful finding. A bounded-efficiency instruction — "begin with the failing test and likely implementation files, inspect more only when evidence requires it, make the smallest sufficient change, run the relevant tests, and stop when the acceptance criteria pass" — is neutral or better on all six holdout models. It **does not reduce observed diagnosis, evidence collection, or final validation.** It declines to authorize work the task doesn't require.

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** Prompt engineering for agents is not wording optimization. It's the design of what work the agent is asked to perform, how that work propagates through tools, and when it should stop. A short prompt that says "explore multiple approaches" is more expensive than a long prompt that says "make the smallest sufficient change."

2. **The non-obvious implication:** Your harness is an amplifier. A wasteful instruction that costs 2× in a lean harness might cost 30× in a verbose one, because the static prefix, accumulated context, and tool policies multiply the waste at every turn. Harness prefix size is a direct cost multiplier.

3. **Essential knowledge.** If you're building coding agents or any tool-using agent, this paper is directly actionable. Write bounded-efficiency instructions. Measure your harness's static prefix. Monitor branch count and verification loop count. These are the actual levers for controlling agent cost, and they're cheaper to fix than anything else in your pipeline.

## 15. Rehearse: Stepping Back from the Confidence Cliff

**Source:** Tencent — arxiv 2607.27687
**Core discovery:** In autoresearch loops (LLM proposes ML code changes, runs training, keeps improvements), the agent's ability to judge which proposals are worth running degrades as successful changes accumulate. It commits just as readily while getting dramatically less reliable.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Autoresearch loops (AutoSOTA, AIDE, AlphaEvolve, Karpathy's nanochat) follow a simple pattern: propose a modification, run a full training job, keep it if the metric improves. The assumption: if the agent can generate good ideas, and we have a clear metric, the loop will converge.

The reality from public AutoSOTA logs: the fraction of helpful modifications falls from **70% in iterations 1-2 to 43% by iteration 6+**, and their mean gain shrinks from **3.6% to 0.3%**. Late in the loop, most training jobs are wasted — they produce changes that get reverted.

### The Confidence Cliff

The paper names and measures a specific degradation pattern:

**Selective accuracy** (accuracy of the pre-execution judgment when the judge returns a verdict) collapses from **82.8% at cold start to 56.9%** once three or more successes have accumulated.

**Coverage** (fraction of cases where the judge returns a verdict) **rises from 76% to 85%** over the same period.

The judge becomes **more willing to commit while becoming less reliable.** It doesn't know what it doesn't know. This is the **confidence cliff** — the agent's judgment quality falls off a cliff while its confidence stays high or increases.

This has two contributors: useful changes become rarer (the easy gains are taken), AND the agent becomes less reliable at judging which remaining changes are worth a run. Rehearse targets the judgment component.

### The Rehearse Skill

Rehearse is a lightweight modification to the autoresearch loop:

1. **Propose several modifications** (not just one)
2. **Compare them before execution** using a pre-run pairwise judgment
3. **Run only the most promising** candidate
4. **Record what changed and whether it worked** — a focused outcome memory
5. **Next judgment sees only the few most similar records** — focused retrieval, not full trace

The key intervention is **focused outcome memory.** Instead of showing the judge the entire optimization history (which overwhelms it as depth increases), show it only the few most similar past changes and their outcomes. This raises late selective accuracy from 56.9% to **83.5%**.

**Why unfocused history fails:** Full trace history overwhelms the judge with irrelevant modifications. Summary-based history compresses away the specific details the judge needs. Focused retrieval (candidate-conditioned, similarity-based) gives the judge exactly the relevant precedents.

### Results

Across 4,000 budgeted training runs over three loops:
- Rehearse improves the endpoint on **nanochat** (language modeling), **image classification**, and **time-series forecasting** under the same training-run budget
- The explicit selection step (compare before running) beats taking the first proposal

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** Your autoresearch loop has a hidden degradation mode. The agent doesn't just produce fewer useful modifications late in the run — it also becomes worse at judging which modifications to try. If you're not measuring pre-execution judgment accuracy as a function of loop depth, you have no visibility into this failure.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** Memory format matters for judgment quality. Full history is worse than no history for late-stage decisions (it overwhelms the judge). Summary history loses critical details. Focused retrieval — showing only the most relevant prior modifications and their outcomes — is the sweet spot. This principle generalizes beyond autoresearch to any agent that makes decisions informed by growing history.

3. **Useful context.** If you're running any kind of iterative optimization loop with LLMs (autoresearch, code evolution, prompt tuning), this paper tells you the loop will degrade in a specific, measurable way and gives you a specific, tested fix. Not essential for everyone, but essential if you're doing iterative ML improvement.

## 16. ContinualSkillBench: Can LLM Agents Truly Evolve Their Capabilities?

**Source:** Peking University / BIGAI — arxiv 2608.03874
**Core question:** Agent frameworks let models build skill libraries over time. But does this actually work? Can agents consolidate experience into robust, transferable skills — or do they just accumulate junk?

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Agent skill systems (Claude Code's CLAUDE.md, Codex's project context, Voyager's skill library) assume that agents can build useful skill libraries through interaction. But nobody tested whether skill evolution works across a sustained sequence of tasks with realistic difficulty progression, cross-task dependencies, and opportunities for reuse.

Previous benchmarks either tested agents on isolated tasks with fixed skill documents (SkillsBench), studied single-task skill generation (SkillLearnBench), or examined short task sequences (CL-Bench). None evaluated whether skills acquired from early tasks can be consolidated and reused on **different** downstream tasks across diverse domains.

### The Core Mechanism

ContinualSkillBench constructs five domain-specific task streams (Law, Healthcare, Finance, Math, Office), each containing 100 interconnected subtasks. Tasks are ordered by increasing difficulty and potential for skill reuse, with downstream tasks implicitly relying on skills from earlier ones.

**The protocol:** After completing each subtask, the agent receives feedback and may create or modify skills in its repository. The updated repository is available for subsequent tasks. This creates a realistic progressive learning environment.

**Three execution modes compared:**
- **Independent:** Every task from scratch, no memory, no skills — the floor
- **Sequential (In-Context):** Full interaction history available but no explicit skill library
- **Sequential (Skill Maintenance):** Agent explicitly creates, updates, and retrieves skills

### Results That Matter

**Sequential execution generally helps.** It improves normalized reward in 14 of 15 model-domain combinations, yielding an aggregate relative gain of 16.9%. But gains vary substantially across models and domains.

**In-context learning matches explicit skill maintenance on average.** 0.605 vs. 0.602 normalized reward across the three ablation domains. Most of the improvement comes from **adaptation to prior context and feedback** rather than reusable skill abstraction. The skills are a side effect, not the cause.

**But skills provide selective benefits** for tasks requiring reusable procedures or precise outputs. They help when the task genuinely needs a previously-learned procedure applied exactly.

**Less capable models accumulate more skills of lower quality.** GPT-4o accumulates 384 skills across five domains (vs. 205 for GPT-5.3-Codex), but these skills are less frequently reused and receive lower quality scores. Weaker models over-fragment their skill libraries — creating many narrow, task-specific skills instead of consolidating into general, reusable ones.

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** Skill evolution is not free productivity. Most of the gain from sequential execution comes from in-context adaptation, not from the skill library itself. If your agent is already seeing its previous interactions in context, adding an explicit skill maintenance system may add complexity without proportional benefit.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** Weaker models are worse at skill abstraction, not just at task execution. They create fragmented, over-specific skills that don't transfer. If you're using a weaker model with a skill system, you might be accumulating a junk drawer of rarely-reused procedures. Monitor skill reuse rate, not just skill count.

3. **Useful context.** Important for anyone building agents with persistent skill libraries. The headline: skill systems work, but most of their value might already be captured by simply keeping interaction history in context. Explicit skills are worth the complexity only when tasks require precise procedural reuse.

## 17. MerchantBench: Long-Term Coherence in E-Commerce Operations

**Source:** arxiv 2607.28956
**Core problem:** Agent benchmarks test bounded tasks with clear completion criteria. But real-world agent deployments require sustained decision-making over months, where earlier actions constrain later options and consequences emerge only after many intervening decisions.

### What Existed Before and What Broke

Existing commerce benchmarks cover shopping interactions (WebShop), storefront navigation, customer support, or short competitive episodes (Market-Bench). None test continuous operation of the same online store over a realistic time horizon with persistent state, cash constraints, and delayed feedback.

### The Core Mechanism

MerchantBench formulates seller-side e-commerce as a **partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP)** over 365 simulated days:

- **Product Catalog:** 98,843 real e-commerce product records with full-year demand trajectories
- **Order-Level Simulation:** Daily demand generates hourly order arrivals. Each order progresses through: Placed → Procured → Shipped → Delivered → Settled, with exception events (canceled, stockout, delay, return, dispute)
- **Agent Actions:** Product sourcing, listing/pricing control, cash flow management, monitoring
- **Temporal Asymmetry:** Listing decisions commit cash immediately, but fulfillment failures and after-sales outcomes become observable only later. The agent must associate later outcomes with earlier decisions
- **Decision Frequency:** Agent acts every 12 simulated hours across 8,760 hourly steps
- **Termination:** Operation ends when the security deposit is exhausted by unpaid fines

**Four interdependent decision components:**
1. **Product Sourcing:** Identify products to list from the 98K catalog based on demand signals
2. **Listing and Pricing Control:** Set prices that balance margin against demand volume
3. **Cash Flow Management:** Cash funds procurement and covers losses — over-committing breaks the business
4. **Mixed-Latency Feedback Adaptation:** Use shorter supplier feedback and delayed order outcomes to revise strategy

### Results That Matter

**The human-AI gap is enormous.** Human participants (non-experts) achieved mean final net assets of **217.61K RMB.** The best LLM configuration (Qwen3.7-Max under Hermes) reached **59.46K RMB** — 27.3% of human. The best under ReAct (GPT-5.6 Sol) reached only **40.89K RMB** — 18.8% of human.

**Framework matters as much as model.** Averaged across all eight models, Hermes produces **53.3% higher final net assets**, 71.5% higher GMV, and 71.2% more orders than ReAct. Seven of eight models perform better under Hermes. The sole exception: Kimi K2.6 scores 4.1% lower under Hermes, showing that framework benefits depend on the underlying model.

**Model ranking is unstable across frameworks.** GPT-5.6 Sol leads under ReAct; Qwen3.7-Max leads under Hermes. Qwen3.7-Max has the highest absolute performance but also the highest variability (55.1% coefficient of variation across runs vs. 3.3% for GPT-5.6 Sol under ReAct).

### Two Failure Modes: Operational and Strategic Coherence

The paper introduces a diagnostic framework for long-horizon agent failures:

**Operational Coherence** — does the agent keep working?

Human operators maintain a **100% Sustained Window Rate (SWR)** — they act in every scheduled decision window across the full year. LLM agents range from **10.6% to 99.4%** under ReAct and **17.8% to 66.1%** under Hermes. Many agents progressively reduce store intervention over time. Qwen3.7-Max's quarterly Effective Window Rate drops from 62% to 37% under Hermes and from 68% to 23% under ReAct.

Two specific patterns:
- **Control-Loop Narrowing:** The agent's activity gradually collapses into reactive handling of upstream supplier events (price changes, stockouts) with no self-initiated sourcing, repricing, or diagnosis. For ReAct Qwen3.7-Max, supply chain checks rise from 14% to 34% of its remaining tool calls as all other activity decays.
- **Premature Abandonment:** The agent concludes the store cannot recover, although feasible actions remain. In one Hermes Kimi K2.6 run, the agent made this judgment on Day 104 and then took **no environment action in 355 of the remaining 523 decision windows.**

**Strategic Coherence** — does the agent pursue the right objective with evidence-calibrated adaptation?

- **Goal Consistency** fails when agents lose focus on maximizing terminal net assets. They get distracted by reactive firefighting and stop making proactive sourcing and pricing decisions.
- **Evidence-Calibrated Adaptation** fails when agents don't adjust strategy as liquidity, demand, and experience change. Human operators broaden procurement price range as cash grows (mean active listing prices increase from RMB 43-53 in Q1 to RMB 59-91 in Q4). GLM-5.2, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and Kimi K2.6 maintain flat listing price trajectories — they don't capitalize on growing liquidity.

**Portfolio alignment over time:** Human operators' monthly portfolio alignment with actual demand rises from 56.1 in June to above 80 in December-January. Rule-based baseline stays near the catalog median. LLM improvements are weaker or inconsistent. Claude Opus 4.8 achieves reasonable alignment later, but simultaneously contracts its shelf from 37.3 to 12.0 products — alignment alone without portfolio breadth doesn't produce profit improvement.

### The Honest Take

1. **What most engineers miss:** Your agent might ace bounded tasks but fail catastrophically at sustained operation. The skills required for long-term coherence — temporal credit assignment, portfolio management under uncertainty, cash flow discipline, and the discipline to keep working even when things look bad — are fundamentally different from the skills tested by existing benchmarks. The 18.8-27.3% human parity numbers should concern anyone planning autonomous agent deployments.

2. **The non-obvious implication:** Delayed feedback creates a temporal credit assignment problem that current agents don't solve. When an outcome appears 30 days after the decision that caused it, the agent has made hundreds of intervening decisions. But equally important: agents exhibit **activity decay** — they gradually stop engaging with the environment over long horizons, either narrowing to reactive patterns or abandoning the task entirely. This isn't a reasoning failure; it's a persistence failure.

3. **Useful context.** Not essential for most agent engineers today, but important for anyone building agents that need to operate autonomously over extended periods with persistent state and financial constraints. The Operational vs. Strategic Coherence framework is a useful diagnostic for any long-horizon agent, not just e-commerce.

## 18. Synthesis: What These 17 Papers Tell You About Building Agents

Here's what emerges when you read all 17 papers as a single story rather than isolated contributions.

### Mental Model 1: The Three-Layer Architecture Is Not Optional

Papers 1, 2, 7, 8, 12, and 14 converge on the same conclusion from different angles: agent reliability is a **harness + loop + graph** problem, not a model problem.

- **CockpitHAT** (Paper 1) shows that correctness collapses without process-level attribution → you need the **loop** (evidence-based verification)
- **Master Architecture** (Paper 2) formalizes the three layers explicitly
- **Argus** (Paper 7) shows structured state management beats raw context → you need the **harness** (state persistence)
- **EvoHarness-RL** (Paper 8) shows harness usage should be learned, not scripted
- **Harness-R1** (Paper 12) shows harness editing is a learnable skill and gains stack with model training
- **Same Task, Different Work** (Paper 14) shows the prompt is part of the harness, and it's a cost multiplier

The mental model: **Harness = Environment, Loop = Feedback, Graph = Flow.** Miss any one and your agent is a demo.

### Mental Model 2: Evidence Over Sentences

Papers 1, 2, 5, 9, 11, and 14 all enforce the same principle from different angles:

- **"All tests should pass now"** is a sentence. **"pytest: 27 passed, 0 failed"** is evidence. (Paper 2)
- **"The answer is correct"** is a model's assertion. **Tracing the dependency path** reveals whether it's actually correct. (Paper 1)
- **"The agent failed"** is useless. **"TOOL — MODEL · fault: MODEL"** is actionable. (Paper 9)
- **Majority vote** (counting evidence) beats **model selection** (model's sentence about which answer is best). (Paper 11)
- **A path through a graph** is structural evidence. **A similar chunk** is proximity. (Paper 5)

Production rule: if a signal comes from a model's text output, treat it as a hypothesis, not a fact. If it comes from a deterministic external system, treat it as evidence.

### Mental Model 3: Memory Degrades Without Maintenance — But Maintenance Is Harder Than You Think

Papers 3, 6, 10, and 16 tell a coherent story about agent memory:

- **Flat graphs** fail because updates are local but dependencies are global → **HiGram** (Paper 3) introduces hierarchical, dependency-aware editing
- **Filesystem memory** works, but organizational quality erodes as the store grows → only the strongest management agents hold the contract (Paper 6)
- **LLM-generated memory** is expensive and lossy → **Zero-Mem** (Paper 10) shows structured views over raw traces beat abstractions
- **Skill libraries** accumulate junk → weaker models create more skills of lower quality (Paper 16)

The pattern: memory systems have a **build-up → degradation → cleanup** lifecycle. Without explicit maintenance interventions (preservation rules, focused retrieval, periodic consolidation), all memory systems decay. The agent will corrupt its own memory if unsupervised.

### Mental Model 4: Budget-Awareness Is the Meta-Skill

Papers 8, 11, 14, and 15 all converge on cost-conscious agent behavior:

- **EvoHarness-RL** (Paper 8): Harness usage drops during training as the agent internalizes cheap behaviors — it learns when scaffolding is worth its cost
- **Sample More, Reflect Less** (Paper 11): Self-reflection is a worse use of tokens than another attempt
- **Same Task, Different Work** (Paper 14): "Explore multiple approaches" costs 2.4-7.4× with zero success gain
- **Rehearse** (Paper 15): Propose several modifications, compare before execution, run only the most promising — pre-run filtering saves compute

The mental model: **every agent action has an implicit token cost, and the right comparison is always against the simplest use of those same tokens.** An action is worth taking only if it produces more value than the tokens it consumes would produce via the cheapest effective alternative.

### Mental Model 5: Long-Horizon Coherence Is the Unsolved Problem

Papers 15, 16, and 17 reveal the hard frontier:

- **Rehearse** (Paper 15): Pre-execution judgment degrades at loop depth → the agent gets worse at its own decisions over time
- **ContinualSkillBench** (Paper 16): Skill libraries don't reliably consolidate → in-context adaptation is often enough, explicit skills add limited value
- **MerchantBench** (Paper 17): Best LLM reaches 27.3% of human performance over 365 simulated days → temporal credit assignment over long horizons is fundamentally unsolved

The honest assessment: current agents are excellent at bounded tasks (hours, single sessions) and mediocre-to-bad at sustained operations (days, weeks, months). The gap is not model intelligence — it's temporal coherence, state management across long horizons, and the ability to connect delayed outcomes to earlier decisions.

### The Production Checklist That Falls Out of These Papers

| Layer | What to Build | What Paper Teaches Why |
|-------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Harness** | State persistence, file caching, sandbox, security | Papers 2, 6, 7 |
| **Loop** | Deterministic evidence checks, budget caps, retry limits | Papers 1, 2, 11 |
| **Graph** | Parallel fan-out, evidence-based routing | Papers 2, 5 |
| **Memory** | Hierarchical store with preservation rules, focused retrieval | Papers 3, 6, 10 |
| **Evaluation** | Process-level attribution, dependency-graph tracing | Papers 1, 9 |
| **Prompts** | Bounded-efficiency instructions, no "explore multiple approaches" | Paper 14 |
| **Optimization** | Pre-run filtering, budget-aware scaffolding | Papers 8, 11, 15 |
| **Monitoring** | Skill reuse rate, judgment accuracy vs. depth, harness prefix size | Papers 15, 16, 14 |

### The Honest Final Take

If you read one paper: **Paper 9** (Failure Taxonomy). It gives you a diagnostic framework that makes every other paper's lessons immediately applicable. When your agent fails, you need to know whether to fix the model, the harness, or the environment — and 41 named failure modes with edge + fault-side localization is the best tool for that decision that currently exists.

If you read three papers: Add **Paper 2** (Master Architecture) for the structural foundation, and **Paper 14** (Prompt-Induced Waste) for the most immediately cost-saving intervention.

If you're building something that needs to last: Read **Papers 6, 15, and 17**. They tell you how memory, judgment, and coherence degrade over time — the failure modes you won't see in your first demo but will see in your first production week.

The field is not model-limited for most production use cases. It is harness-limited, memory-limited, and evaluation-limited. The papers that matter most are the ones that improve the scaffolding around the model, not the model itself.
